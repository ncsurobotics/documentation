## Overview

Documentation is something you’ve surely run into before. Very likely, it was not your favorite part of the project. However, keeping track of what has been done, what needs to be done, and the reasons why decisions are made is crucial for long-term project organization. Since AquaPack does documentation online, documentation is useful for storing links to reference websites (for specific parts to be obtained or to list options for a part) as well as providing clear images of the status of the project (through screenshots of CAD models or pictures of sketches).

## Purpose

Every project has steps, and each step of the design and manufacturing processes includes decisions. These decisions are (theoretically) made for good reasons based on physical or manufacturing constraints in mind. Documenting the decision and especially the reason for said decision is important. Documentation allows you to reference why you made a decision when you need to change something with your design. Design doesn’t happen all at once, and sometimes it will have been several months since you last looked at your project. Good documentation provides a useful refresher so that you don’t lose track of a crucial design constraint.  Documentation also enables you to keep your project organized. Knowing what design constraints and parts to work on is very useful so that things don’t slip through the cracks. Finally, solid documentation allows people to see the progress on the project and see other relevant information. Other people working on separate projects may need to reference a link you found or other information you gathered while working on your project, and having organized documentation makes that process much easier.

## Process & Sections

A few years ago, a wonderful Mech Lead named Alex Ofsanik (Mechanical Lead 2024–2025; all hail!) made an amazing [template for documentation](https://docs.google.com/document/d/19NRCqURfsxc77LBLSn6V_6IvIeJadBH6tICFQv0PLbc/edit?tab=t.0). It has sections laid out and explanations for what to do in each section. The section that is used meeting to meeting is the Work Completed section. This is where you will put in what you did each meeting. This section is the most important section, as it is the meat to the entire documentation file. To start a new day in the section, all you have to do is go to the bottom of the section and type out the date. The date is important so that the entries can be organized and so that people can see how recently the project was worked on. Underneath the date, you put a bulleted list of whatever it is you did that meeting (because I wholeheartedly believe that full sentences are evil when you can get your point across just fine with a beautiful list). It might include links to websites you visited for ideas or for specific parts, images of design (which is super helpful during the design of a 3D modeled part, especially an isometric view), or simply just brief notes and reasonings for the decisions made that meeting.

Other sections include Scope, Timeline, and Required Components. The first one, Scope, is where you put a brief explanation of what the project even is. This should include what the project is, why it is on the robot or why it is important, and a general idea of the steps of the project. The second section is an estimated timeline. At the beginning of the project, set up a vague timeline of when you will (hopefully) complete each part of the project. As time goes on, update it (maybe like once a month or so?). Keeping a semi-solid idea of where you are in the project is helpful for overall club timekeeping, since some projects are required for progress on the actual robot. The third section is a table called Required Components that includes the parts and pieces that are needed for the project to actually be built. This includes what the item is, the purpose of the item, the cost of the item(s) (ideally with a link to be able to find it later), and how many of the items are needed (this also makes it much easier to add to the master parts list for the robot later). Don’t forget to include your name(s) on the document as well! This is important so that we can bug you later on if people have questions about why something was done one way or another (also ensures we know who to bug if you don’t do your documentation)

Every time the project is worked on, even if it is just a little, make an entry. I know it will likely be boring and tedious, but good documentation is very useful when it needs to be referenced later. Once or twice, I’ve had to look at old documentation (like, at least a year old) to find some bit of information to move forward with my project, and maintaining your documentation will allow people in the future (maybe even you) find the information they need. While returning members know a lot, they don’t know everything about the robot, and keeping track of details can be surprisingly useful later.
