## International Standards

Most things in engineering are governed by some sort of national or international standard, and hardware is no exception. There are official standards for what exactly constitutes an M3 screw, a 10/32 nut, or a gear with some stated pitch or diameter. The two most common for our work are ISO (for metric) and ANSI (for imperial).

## Metric Bolts

Metric bolt sizes are defined by three parameters: diameter, length, and thread pitch. Diameter is usually given in millimeters, and refers to the nominal major diameter. Length is usually given in millimeters and refers to the length of the bolt. Length does NOT include the head of the bolt, and only measures the thread & (if they exist) shoulder sections of a bolt. Pitch refers to the height of an individual thread.

![Metric bolt diagram labeling major diameter, pitch, and length](/mechanical/quickstart/metric-bolt-dimensions.jpg)

*Fig. 1 Metric Bolt Diagram*

Let’s look at some examples! Say someone says they need an M3-0.5 x 18 bolt. What does that mean? Well, the first number is the bolts nominal diameter (3mm). The second is the pitch (0.5 mm, indicating that each thread wrap takes 0.5 mm of height). The third indicates that the bolt is 18 mm long!

Alright, what about an M10-1.25 x 50? Well, it has a 10mm nominal major diameter, a pitch of 1.25mm, and is 50mm long!

![Two hex-head bolts showing the heads, shoulders, and threaded shanks](/mechanical/quickstart/hex-head-bolts.jpg)

Note that most metric diameters have two common thread types— a course threading, and a fine threading. If no thread pitch is given, you can assume the course threading, as it is by far the most common. A chart of pitches can be found at the bottom of this document.

## Imperial Bolts

Imperial bolts function similarly to metric bolts, but use tpi (threads per inch) instead of pitch. Imperial diameters under ¼ inch are given as a number between 0 and 10 rather than as their actual diameter (a chart with these conversions is provided at the end of the document).

![Imperial bolt diagram showing diameter, threads per inch, and length](/mechanical/quickstart/imperial-bolt-dimensions.jpg)

*Fig. 2 Imperial Bolt Diagram*

So, once again, let's practice! If you have a ¼”-20 x 2”, then you have a ¼ inch diameter bolt with 20 threads per inch that is 2 inches long! If you have a #10-32 x 1.5, then you have a #10 bolt (0.19” nominal diameter) with 32 threads per inch that is 1.5” long.

## Washers

Washers go in between bolts and nuts, or between materials and bolt heads. They can serve a number of purposes, but their most basic use is to distribute the force of a bolt over a larger area, allowing you to tighten the bolt more before damaging a material. Specialized types of washers can also be used to reduce vibration, prevent bolts from loosening, and even reduce corrosion by interrupting galvanic circuits. Washers are particularly useful if you need to tightly bolt a delicate surface, such as wood or acrylic, as they spread out loads and reduce the risk of cracking something!
